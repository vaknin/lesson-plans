## A. Time

### A1. What the research gives us to measure with

The three course files are silent on minutes. So I used four outside yardsticks.

- **Y1. How many class responses fit in a minute.** MacSuga-Gage & Simonsen (2015) reviewed 15 studies of teacher-directed "opportunities to respond" (one teacher prompt plus the class's answer).
  - Class-wide drill of basic facts worked at about 3 to 5 per minute.
  - The natural rate in 35 ordinary elementary classrooms averaged 2.62 per minute (Stichter et al., 2009, as reported in the review).
  - Class-wide formats that need more than a spoken answer ran at about 0.9 to 1.2 per minute.
  - These are US classrooms and not EFL. I use the rates as a ruler, not a law.
- **Y2. Time lost inside teaching time.** Rosenshine (1981), on the Beginning Teacher Evaluation Study, grades 2 and 5.
  - About 20% of in-class time was non-instructional. Most of it was transitions (35 of 45 minutes a day).
  - Inside time already allocated to a subject, 7 to 10 minutes per hour still went to "interim activities and waiting". The paper calls this "a fact of current classroom life that applies to even the most efficient of classrooms".
  - Scaled to 30 minutes: 3.5 to 5 minutes.
- **Y3. How much of a period is teaching.** OECD (2019), TALIS 2018.
  - Teachers report 78% of class time on actual teaching and learning, 13% on keeping order, 8% on administration (OECD average, lower secondary).
  - In most systems with primary data, the typical primary teacher is below 80%.
  - Teachers with 5 years' experience or less get about 3 minutes less per 60-minute lesson.
  - I did not look up Israel's own figure.
- **Y4. Wait time.** Rowe (1986): teachers typically wait less than one second after a question. Recall stages need a few seconds of silence, so they cannot be run at drill speed. [inferred]

### A2. Is "40 minutes minus 10" right?

Yes.

- 78% of 40 minutes is 31 minutes (Y3). For a newer teacher it is about 29 to 30.
- So 30 net minutes matches the best data available.
- But Y2 says another 3.5 to 5 minutes leak away *inside* those 30, in stage changes and waiting. The design gives all 30 minutes to activities. So each stage's minutes must also pay for its own set-up and demonstration.
- The lesson has 14 stages, so 13 stage changes: one every 2 minutes 18 seconds. That is the main time risk.

### C2. Against peer-reviewed research: mixed, so do not overclaim

The course sources tell you to use context. Research on whether a story helps children *remember the words* is not on your side.

- **Goossens et al. (2014), which the LP already cites.** 60 Dutch third graders learned 20 hard words. Half met them in a story that was stopped after each sentence to explain the new word. Half met them as plain word pairs. A week later "the children in the word pairs condition outperformed the children in the story condition".
  - That story procedure is the same shape as stage 3.
  - Limits: the words were in the pupils' first language, were taught by synonym, and the test looked like the word-pairs condition. The authors say this themselves.
- **Laufer & Shmueli (1997).** Words presented in lists and in single sentences "were remembered better than words presented in text and elaborated text".
- **Webb (2007).** Japanese EFL students learned words in a glossed sentence or as pairs. No significant difference. The abstract adds that research "has generated little evidence indicating that context facilitates vocabulary learning".
  - Neither Laufer & Shmueli nor Webb studied young children. [inferred]
- **What does support the design:**
  - Reusing the *same* story. Three-year-olds learned new words when one storybook was read three times, and did not when three different stories carried the same words (Horst, Parsons & Bryan, 2011). First-language, much younger children.
  - More mentions of a word in a story mean more learning (Flack, Field & Horst, 2018, a meta-analysis of 38 studies with pre-readers).
  - Stopping to explain each word while reading. Elley (1989), with 7- and 8-year-olds, found this raised gains a lot. [inferred]
  - A case study of story-based German lessons for ages 10–11 found pupils recalled many words and sentences (Kirsch, 2016). One class, no comparison group.

What this means for tomorrow:

- Stage 3 is right to stop at each word and give it a picture, a gesture and three repetitions. That is the "word pair" part, and it is the part the evidence favours. Gestures and pictures have direct support at this age (Andrä et al., 2020). Do not shorten it to save the story.
- Using one story three times (3, 10, 12) is better supported than three sets of sentences.
- In the LP and in the exam, justify the story by F3 s.9 and F2 s.6, by the discourse principle (L-F p. 131) and by the evaluation form. Do not say research shows words are learned more easily in a story. SKILL.md says context makes words "easier to learn"; the studies above do not show that.

### C3. Problems in the story text for Pre-A1

**1. Nearly half the story starts with S.**

| | Count |
|---|---|
| Running words in the 7 lines | 41 |
| Target-word tokens: snake 5, star 2, stop 2, school 1, scissors 1, sad 1 | 12 |
| Other words starting with S: Sam 4, Snip 2, Sssss 1 | 7 |
| All words starting with S | 19 (46%) |

- In stage 10 the rule is "hiss and do the gesture whenever they hear an S word", and "Sam" counts. Line 1 alone has three: "This is **S**am. **S**am goes to **s**chool."
- The sound /s/ also ends "This" and "cuts". The rule "hear S" cannot separate these from the targets.
- So the task drifts from "recognise the 6 words" (what the MoE row asks) to "notice the sound /s/". Only the gesture shows which word a pupil recognised. "Sam" has no gesture, so the gesture separates the two cleanly.
- Thirty children hissing is noise in the same range as the sound they are listening for. [inferred] Classroom noise hurts children's speech perception and listening comprehension more than adults'; this was shown with first and third graders (Klatte, Lachmann & Meis, 2010). Listening in a foreign language makes it harder still (García Lecumberri, Cooke & Cutler, 2010). [inferred]
- If the teacher speaks over the hiss, the next word is lost. If the teacher waits, the stage runs over (A3).
- Fix at 0 minutes: change 3.

**2. The panels on screen give the answer away.** In stage 10 the star panel is showing when the teacher says "star". A pupil can respond from the picture. This is the same issue as the corner hint in stage 4 (first review, F6). The pictures are needed for understanding, so I propose no change. The *timing* of the hiss still needs listening.

**3. The snake is sad.** The story ties "sad" to the snake.

- If the "sad" picture in stages 4, 5, 11 and 13 is the sad snake, then "Is it a snake?" and "Is it sad?" are both true of it.
- In the pair game, a pupil whose secret word is *snake* can truthfully answer "Yes, it is!" to "Is it sad?".
- Controlled practice needs one right answer (F2 s.10–11).
- Use single-thing pictures for the six words: for "sad", a sad face. I could not see the new pictures. [inferred]
- The same check applies to stage 12 (a). The star panel may also show scissors. The stop panel shows a snake. The last panel shows a star and a snake. Choose three panels where each target thing appears once. [inferred]

**4. "Sam" and "sad".**

- They differ by one last sound and one last letter. "Sam" is heard 4 times in the first three lines, before "sad" is ever heard.
- In stage 12 the written lines start with "Sam". Pupils who read by the first letters (Ehri, 2005) may read "Sam" as "sad". [inferred]
- "Sam" also sounds like the Hebrew word סם ("drug"). Some third graders may giggle. Carry on without comment. [inferred]
- One gain: "Sam", "Stop" and "Snip" show capital S beside small s. That serves a can-do the LP already lists: "Can recognize the letters of the alphabet in upper and lower case".
- The story name is fixed, so I propose no change to it. Change 3 keeps "Sam" apart from the six words.

**5. "Is it sad?" and "Is it stop?".** "Is it sad?" is correct English. It describes a thing; it does not name one, so it sits oddly beside "Is it a star?". "Is it stop?" is not natural English. The first review covered the missing "a" (F11) and the closing fix in stage 14 handles it. The story helps here: "Stop, snake! Stop!" uses *stop* for its real job.

**6. Words that are not being taught.**

- 18 of the 41 running words are neither target words nor known from the B lesson: this, goes, to, has, snip, cuts, look, the, no, friends, here, for, you, happy. Some may be known from class routines. [inferred]
- For unsupported listening, adults needed to know 90–95% of the words (van Zeeland & Schmitt, 2013). Here the pupils know about half.
- So understanding must come from the pictures, the gestures and the voice. This fits the can-do the LP quotes: language "delivered clearly and slowly in a well-defined, familiar, everyday context".
- Two results. The telling cannot be rushed, which is why stage 3 runs over. And the long lines should not be echo-read in stage 12.

**7. Lines 6 and 7 are long.** Line 6 is 8 words. Line 7 is 10 words, with no new target word. They are fine to hear. They are too long to echo-read for pupils still learning the alphabet (F3 s.9 "easy to difficult"). [inferred]

**8. The words get unequal turns in the story.** Snake is said 5 times; school, scissors and sad once each. More mentions mean more learning (Flack et al., 2018). School and scissors are also the two words whose spelling does not match their sound (first review, F4). Give these three the turns in stages 4 and 5, where not every word fits (change 8).

What the story text gets right: short lines 1 to 5; "Snip, snip!" and "Sssss!" are sounds the class can join; "happy" at the end gives "sad" its opposite; line 7 says "star" and "snake" again.

## E. What contradicts the research, most serious first

1. **High. The plan holds about 32 to 33 minutes of work in 30.** Stage changes and waiting take 7 to 10 minutes per hour even in efficient classrooms (Rosenshine, 1981), and choral work runs at 3 to 5 responses a minute (MacSuga-Gage & Simonsen, 2015). The stages that pay are the last three. One of them is the only communicative practice in the lesson (L-F p. 129; F3 s.9). The "if late" list saves under 1 minute. Changes 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 9. [inferred]

2. **Medium–high. Stage 10 asks for a response on 46% of the words, and the response is noise.** The MoE row asks pupils to "recognize the oral word in a sentence". The rule "hear S" turns that into noticing a sound. The hiss masks the next words for the listeners least able to cope with noise (Klatte et al., 2010). Change 3.

3. **Medium. Stage 12 does four jobs in 2 minutes and asks beginners to echo-read 41 words.** Lines 6 and 7 are 8 and 10 words long. This is against "easy to difficult" (F3 s.9) and against what is known about early word reading (Ehri, 2005). In (b), the letter frame makes reading unnecessary, against SKILL.md. Changes 4 and 5.

4. **Medium. The reading half of the objective has almost no evidence left.** Cutting Read and do removed the only step where a written word alone is read for meaning. The B2 Production row now rests on one item. Change 7.

5. **Medium–low. The story is supported by the course sources but not by word-learning studies.** The LP's own cited study found the story group did worse (Goossens et al., 2014). So did Laufer & Shmueli (1997). The lesson is safe because each word is still taught with a picture, a gesture and repetition. The risk is in what is *claimed*. Change 13.

6. **Low–medium. Two sets of sentences share the sentence strands.** The chunk has no Recognition step of its own. The story lines have no spoken Repetition by pupils. F3 s.9's stages build on one item. Change 12 (wording only).

7. **Low–medium. The teacher talks more.** Two tellings and a read-aloud add about 2 minutes of teacher voice to a 30-minute lesson. F2 s.8 asks for the minimum. No change proposed: the story is fixed. Keep the telling brisk. [inferred]

8. **Low. "Sad" and the snake.** If the pictures are story panels, two answers can be true at once. That breaks controlled practice (F2 s.10–11). Change 10. [inferred]

9. **Low. Transitions and praise are not written.** Two evaluation-form items and a SKILL.md rule. Change 11.

10. **Low. Three LP sentences cite a source for something it does not say, and five more cite loosely.** D1. Change 12.

What the revised lesson gets right, with evidence:

- One story used three times, not three sets of sentences (Horst et al., 2011).
- Each word still isolated with picture, gesture and voice inside the story (Andrä et al., 2020).
- Heard before read (F2 s.42).
- The pair game keeps its 5 minutes, is demonstrated first, and uses the same procedure as Peek! (Mackey et al., 2007).
- The rules are restated in Hebrew by a pupil (L-F p. 132; evaluation form).
- Errors are noted and fixed at the end (L-F p. 127, p. 132).
- "Guess first, mime on Yes" keeps the information gap (F2 s.30).
- Stage 8 builds only the three words spelled as they sound (F3 s.9).
