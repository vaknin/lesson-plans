## B. "Guess my mime" scored

### The core problem

In stage 3 the whole class learns one gesture per word. In stage 10 they do the gestures again. In stage 15, A mimes that same gesture. B sees a wiggling hand and knows it is "snake".

- So B's question "Is it a snake?" asks for something B already knows.
- The chapter says such an exchange "is not really communicative" (L-F p. 129, the "Tuesday" example).
- The slides say: "The students don't show each other their information. They have to talk to each other." (F2 s.30). A mime is showing.
- "No, it isn't!" is almost never needed, because B is right the first time. [inferred]

### Morrow's three features (L-F p. 129)

| Feature | As written | Evidence | With the fix below |
|---|---|---|---|
| Information gap | **Partial** | Only A knows the word, so a gap opens. A's mime then closes it without language (F2 s.30; L-F p. 129). | Pass |
| Choice | **Partial** | The chapter means choice of "what she will say and how she will say it" (L-F p. 129). A's choice is shown in mime, not said. B's content is fixed by the mime. B's form is one chunk (or one word in the easier version). | Partial: B truly chooses which word to ask. The form is still fixed. |
| Feedback | **Pass** | B hears "Yes, it is!" / "No, it isn't!". The chapter accepts feedback "on the content" (L-F p. 134). In practice it is nearly always "Yes". [inferred] | Pass |

### The 7 criteria (F2 s.46)

| # | Criterion | As written | Evidence | With the fix |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | More than a single sentence | **Partial** | One question and one answer per round. Easier version: one word each. | Pass: several question–answer turns per round. |
| 2 | Variety of responses; "unpredictable, creative language use" | **Fail** | Two possible answers. After the mime the answer is predictable. Also fails F2 s.45 question 2 ("unpredictable response"). | Partial: still two answer forms, but nobody can predict which. |
| 3 | The WHAT, not the HOW | **Pass** | The goal is to find the word. The teacher does not correct during the task (LP row; L-F p. 132). | Pass |
| 4 | Pair/group work | **Pass** | Every pair plays at once. This "maximizes" talk time (L-F p. 126 obs. 6, p. 130). | Pass |
| 5 | Information gap | **Partial** | See above. | Pass |
| 6 | Desire to communicate | **Partial** | Pupils want to play and mime. B has no need to find out anything. [inferred] | Pass |
| 7 | Purpose to communicate | **Partial** | The purpose (find A's word) is met by watching, before B speaks. | Pass |

Also F2 s.33 ("Pupils may not be limited in their language use") and F2 s.31 ("any of the language they know"): not met, by design.

### Closed or open?

- **Closed.** Pupils "can only use specific language items": 6 words and one chunk (F2 s.31). The LP is right to call it closed.
- The same slide then keeps the name "communicative activity" for tasks done with "any of the language they know". So a closed-gap game sits *before* the communicative end of the continuum.
- That is acceptable at Pre-A1. The slides allow it: "The teacher decides whether to give them a specific structure or just let them use the language they know" (F2 s.34). The LP should just say so plainly (see D).
- An open gap is not realistic for pupils with about 12 English words. [inferred]

### Smallest fix (0 minutes, same game, same screen)

**Guess first. Mime on "Yes".**

1. A secretly picks a word. A does **not** mime yet.
2. B asks: "Is it a star?" (B may do the star gesture while asking, so every pupil still mimes.)
3. A answers "No, it isn't!" until B is right. Then A says "Yes, it is!" and both do the mime.
4. Swap on the bell, as planned.

Why this is enough:

- The gap is now real and is closed only by talking (F2 s.30).
- With 6 words, B needs 3.5 questions per round on average (1 to 6, each equally likely). "No, it isn't!" is now used for real.
- It is the chapter's own pattern: predict, be told right or wrong, then see the reveal (card game L-F p. 123; picture strip L-F p. 134).
- It is the same procedure as Peek! (stage 13), which the class has just done. So the demo is short. Children aged 7–8 had more chances to use feedback in tasks whose procedure they knew (Mackey, Kanganas & Oliver, 2007; see E).

Optional, also 0 minutes: tell pupils that any English guess is fine ("Snake?", "Is it a snake?", "It's a snake!"). The last one is the letter-B chunk. This adds a small choice of form (L-F p. 129; F2 s.31).

## E. Evidence beyond the three course files

I could check the web. Every DOI below was resolved against Crossref on 2026-11-17, and the author, year, title and journal matched. Findings are taken from the abstracts unless marked. None of these studies is from Israel or with Hebrew speakers. None tests a mime game or a single 40-minute lesson.

### E1. Communicative and information-gap tasks with young beginners

Short answer: yes, there is peer-reviewed evidence for ages 6–10 at very low proficiency. It is thin for ages 7–9 in foreign-language classes. I found no study of information-gap tasks with children who cannot yet read the alphabet.

- Lázaro, A., & Azpilicueta, R. (2015). Investigating negotiation of meaning in EFL children with very low levels of proficiency. *International Journal of English Studies, 15*(1), 1–21. https://doi.org/10.6018/ijes/2015/1/203751
  - Closest match. 8 pairs aged 7–8, EFL, playing a game in class. They negotiated less than older learners, sometimes used L1, and still used a range of strategies. The authors argue for using such pair activities. The end page is from memory. [inferred]
- Mackey, A., Kanganas, A. P., & Oliver, R. (2007). Task familiarity and interactional feedback in child ESL classrooms. *TESOL Quarterly, 41*(2), 285–312. https://doi.org/10.1002/j.1545-7249.2007.tb00060.x
  - Ages 7–8, pairs, second-language (not foreign-language) setting. Pupils used feedback more when the task procedure was familiar. Supports doing Peek! before the pair game with the same procedure.
- Oliver, R. (1998). Negotiation of meaning in child interactions. *The Modern Language Journal, 82*(3), 372–386. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-4781.1998.tb01215.x
  - 96 pairs aged 8–13, ESL, one-way and two-way tasks. Children do negotiate meaning. Adult findings cannot simply be carried over to children.
- Shintani, N. (2012). Input-based tasks and the acquisition of vocabulary and grammar: A process-product study. *Language Teaching Research, 16*(2), 253–279. https://doi.org/10.1177/1362168811431378
  - 15 six-year-old complete beginners in Japan. Listen-and-do tasks, with no demand to speak, raised both receptive and productive word knowledge.
- Shintani, N. (2011). A comparative study of the effects of input-based and production-based instruction on vocabulary acquisition by young EFL learners. *Language Teaching Research, 15*(2), 137–158. https://doi.org/10.1177/1362168810388692
  - 36 Japanese children aged 6–8. Input-based tasks and production-based practice gave similar vocabulary gains. The input-based tasks gave richer interaction.
- Pinter, A. (2007). Some benefits of peer–peer interaction: 10-year-old children practising with a communication task. *Language Teaching Research, 11*(2), 189–207. https://doi.org/10.1177/1362168807074604
  - Hungary, EFL, "very low level of competence", spot-the-differences in pairs. Repeating the task brought visible gains.
- Azkarai, A., & Imaz Agirre, A. (2016). Negotiation of meaning strategies in child EFL mainstream and CLIL settings. *TESOL Quarterly, 50*(4), 844–870. https://doi.org/10.1002/tesq.249
  - 72 children aged 9–12 doing a guessing game and a picture task. The younger ones negotiated more.
- García Mayo, M. P., & Lázaro Ibarrola, A. (2015). Do children negotiate for meaning in task-based interaction? Evidence from CLIL and EFL settings. *System, 54*, 40–54. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.system.2014.12.001
  - Children aged about 8–11 in Spain did negotiate in pair tasks. I could not open the abstract, so this line is from memory. [inferred]
- Butler, Y. G., & Zeng, W. (2014). Young foreign language learners' interactions during task-based paired assessments. *Language Assessment Quarterly, 11*(1), 45–75. https://doi.org/10.1080/15434303.2013.869814
  - 4th graders (9–10) and 6th graders in paired tasks. The 4th graders found joint interaction harder. [inferred]
- Carless, D. (2002). Implementing task-based learning with young learners. *ELT Journal, 56*(4), 389–396. https://doi.org/10.1093/elt/56.4.389
  - Hong Kong primary classes. Four practical problems: noise, mother-tongue use, how many pupils are really involved, and drawing tasks with no language.
- Carless, D. (2004). Issues in teachers' reinterpretation of a task-based innovation in primary schools. *TESOL Quarterly, 38*(4), 639–662. https://doi.org/10.2307/3588283
  - Same setting: mother tongue, discipline and the amount of English produced were the weak points. The end page is from memory. [inferred]
- Copland, F., Garton, S., & Burns, A. (2014). Challenges in teaching English to young learners: Global perspectives and local realities. *TESOL Quarterly, 48*(4), 738–762. https://doi.org/10.1002/tesq.148
  - Survey of 4,459 teachers. Teaching speaking, large classes and discipline are the common difficulties.
- Littlewood, W. (2004). The task-based approach: Some questions and suggestions. *ELT Journal, 58*(4), 319–326. https://doi.org/10.1093/elt/58.4.319
  - I read the full text. It gives a five-step continuum. "Pre-communicative language practice" is question-and-answer "to which everyone knows the answer". "Communicative language practice" is pre-taught language used "to convey information", such as information-gap activities. By this scale, Guess my mime as written is step 2 and with the fix is step 3.
- Butler, Y. G. (2011). The implementation of communicative and task-based language teaching in the Asia-Pacific region. *Annual Review of Applied Linguistics, 31*, 36–57. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0267190511000122
- Littlewood, W. (2007). Communicative and task-based language teaching in East Asian classrooms. *Language Teaching, 40*(3), 243–249. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0261444807004363

### E2. Repetition → recognition → reproduction

Short answer: I found no peer-reviewed study that tests this named sequence. In the course it comes from F3 s.9 and from the MoE form's rows. It is a teaching convention. The parts of it have support:

- **Recognition is easier than recall, so it comes first.** Laufer, B., & Goldstein, Z. (2004). Testing vocabulary knowledge: Size, strength, and computer adaptiveness. *Language Learning, 54*(3), 399–436. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.0023-8333.2004.00260.x
  - 435 learners. The order of difficulty held at every level: passive recognition (easiest), active recognition, passive recall, active recall (hardest). It is a study of test difficulty, not of teaching order.
  - The learners were not young children. [inferred]
- **Reproduction is retrieval practice, and retrieval builds memory.**
  - Goossens, N. A. M. C., Camp, G., Verkoeijen, P. P. J. L., & Tabbers, H. K. (2014). The effect of retrieval practice in primary school vocabulary learning. *Applied Cognitive Psychology, 28*(1), 135–142. https://doi.org/10.1002/acp.2956 — Primary pupils. After one week, words practised by retrieval were recalled better than words restudied. No difference on a recognition test.
  - Barcroft, J. (2007). Effects of opportunities for word retrieval during second language vocabulary learning. *Language Learning, 57*(1), 35–56. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9922.2007.00398.x — Adults, picture–word pairs. A pause to try to recall the word before seeing it gave higher scores up to a week later. This is the "What's missing?" and "Flash!" mechanism.
  - Karpicke, J. D., & Roediger, H. L. (2008). The critical importance of retrieval for learning. *Science, 319*(5865), 966–968. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1152408 — Adults learning foreign word pairs.
- **Recognition stages that only ask pupils to listen and act still teach words.** Shintani (2011, 2012), above. This backs "Stop me when…" and "Stand up if it's true!".
- **Gestures and pictures with the spoken word help children remember.**
  - Andrä, C., Mathias, B., Schwager, A., Macedonia, M., & von Kriegstein, K. (2020). Learning foreign language vocabulary with gestures and pictures enhances vocabulary memory for several months post-learning in eight-year-old school children. *Educational Psychology Review, 32*(3), 815–850. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-020-09527-z — Eight-year-olds, English words. Gestures and pictures both beat audio alone, for up to 6 months. Gestures were not better than pictures.
  - Tellier, M. (2008). The effect of gestures on second language memorisation by young children. *Gesture, 8*(2), 219–235. https://doi.org/10.1075/gest.8.2.06tel — 20 French five-year-olds, 8 English words. Children who reproduced gestures produced more words later.
- **Repeated exposure, meaning in context, interaction, several modes.** Butler, Y. G. (2019). Teaching vocabulary to young second- or foreign-language learners: What can we learn from the research? *Language Teaching for Young Learners, 1*(1), 4–33. https://doi.org/10.1075/ltyl.00003.but — A research review with these four recommendations. The volume and issue numbers are from memory. [inferred]
- **Teaching "Is it a…?" as a fixed chunk.** Myles, F., Hooper, J., & Mitchell, R. (1998). Rote or rule? Exploring the role of formulaic language in classroom foreign language learning. *Language Learning, 48*(3), 323–364. https://doi.org/10.1111/0023-8333.00045 — 16 child beginners of French, followed for 2 years. Rote-learned chunks were later "unpacked" and reused.
- **Presentation → practice → production as a whole.** Anderson, J. (2017). A potted history of PPP with the help of ELT Journal. *ELT Journal, 71*(2), 218–227. https://doi.org/10.1093/elt/ccw055 — Argues that current research no longer rules PPP out. The volume and pages are from memory. [inferred]
- **Reading words when the alphabet is not yet known.** Ehri, L. C. (2005). Learning to read words: Theory, findings, and issues. *Scientific Studies of Reading, 9*(2), 167–188. https://doi.org/10.1207/s1532799xssr0902_4
  - Beginning readers pass through phases. Early on they use partial letter cues, often the first letter. [inferred]
  - All six words here start with S, so that cue cannot tell them apart. [inferred]

Where the course material itself comes from:

- The three phases in File 3 (verbalization, automatization, autonomy) match Ur, P. (1996). *A course in language teaching*. Cambridge University Press, Module 2, Box 2.1. [inferred]
- The pictures of text in File 2 (sentence activities, games) look like pages from Harmer, J. (2012). *Essential teacher knowledge*. Pearson. [unverified]

## F. What contradicts the research, most serious first

1. **High. Stage 15: the mime closes the gap.** B knows the word before asking. This breaks F2 s.30 ("don't show… have to talk") and L-F p. 129. The LP's claim that all three of Morrow's features are present is then not true. Fix: B, 0 minutes.

2. **High. No slack, and the only pair work is at minute 33.** Sixteen stages add up to exactly 40 minutes. There is no time for transitions or for setting up pairs. A lesson with no communicative practice would go against L-F p. 129 and F3 s.9 ("non-communicative to communicative"). Fix: decide tonight which stages to drop if late.
   - If the lesson runs 5 minutes late, the one learner-controlled stage is the one that gets lost. [inferred]

3. **Medium. B4 (stage 14) skips Repetition and uses new sentences.** The fixed rule is all three phases in every strand. The design row for B4 is "Recognition/Reproduction + Production" only. Pupils who are still learning the alphabet must read "A star in the sky." cold. These are also not the sentences drilled in B3. F2 s.42 expects the same language to go from "sentence level oral form to written sentence level". F3 s.9 asks for "easy to difficult".

4. **Medium. Stage 9 asks for spelling the class may not have.** "Scissors" and "school" are not spelled the way they sound. This is against "easy to difficult" (F3 s.9) and against Ehri (2005). Six words in 3 minutes is also 30 seconds a word.
   - Spelling "s-c-i-s-s-o-r-s" aloud needs the names of letters the class may not have learned yet. [inferred]

5. **Medium. Stage 12 does not recognise the B3 chunk.** Stage 11 drills "Is it a…? Yes, it is / No, it isn't". Stage 12 then tests "It's a snake." and the context sentences. So the spoken-sentence strand has no Recognition step for its own target. The phases in F3 s.9 only make sense on the same item.

6. **Low–medium. Stage 4, easier version.** With the target picture in the corner, pupils can match picture to picture and never process the word. That is not recognition of the spoken word. It also breaks Aviv's own rule "Games must make kids think" (SKILL.md). On a projector the hint is shown to everyone, so it is not differentiation.

7. **Low. Two stages called "Production" are not production.** Flash! is one more recall item. Read and do is reading. The labels are the MoE form's. The problem is only the rationale that claims "autonomy" (D2).

8. **Low. Writing, mediation and authentic materials are absent.** The chapter wants all four skills from the start (L-F p. 131). F3 s.9 wants "all 4 modes of communication". These follow from the fixed constraints. The LP already says there is no writing. It should not claim more.

9. **Low. Bare words in five stages.** Stages 5, 6, 8, 9 and 10 practise words with no sentence. F3 s.9 says practice should "be in CONTEXT"; F2 s.6 calls contextualization "a basic principle". Stages 3, 12 and 14 do give context. No change proposed: it would cost time.

10. **Low. No planned L1 moment.** "Judicious" use (L-F p. 132) needs a plan. Young pairs do drift into L1 in tasks (Carless, 2002; Lázaro & Azpilicueta, 2015).
    - The pair-game rules are the likeliest point of confusion. [inferred]

11. **Low. The chunk fits only three of the six words.** "Is it a…?" works for snake, star and school. Scissors, sad and stop take no "a". Such errors are good candidates for the fix in stage 16.
    - Expect "Is it a scissors?" and "Is it a sad?" in stage 15. [inferred]

12. **No change needed. Stage 1.** "What day is it today?" is the chapter's own example of a non-communicative exchange (L-F p. 129). It is fine as a routine. Do not describe it as communication.

What the lesson gets right, with evidence: gestures plus pictures plus voice (Andrä et al., 2020; Tellier, 2008); recall after recognition (Laufer & Goldstein, 2004; Goossens et al., 2014); listen-and-do recognition (Shintani, 2011, 2012); a chunk taught whole (Myles et al., 1998); Peek! as a familiar-procedure rehearsal for the pair game (Mackey et al., 2007); errors noted, then fixed at the end (L-F p. 132).
